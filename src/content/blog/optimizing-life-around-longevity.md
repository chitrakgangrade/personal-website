*Distilling a conversation with a friend about optimizing your life around longevity. Written down on one particular day -- a snapshot of my thinking then, not a permanent contract with myself.*

## The sprint-relax-sprint mindset

As I get older, I'm starting to move away from the sprint-relax-sprint mindset. In my 20s, the model I followed was: go all in on something. Travel like crazy, ignore your health, ignore your career, or go all in on your career and ignore your friends and family because you're distracted with something else.

With time, these things catch up, and you start to question the why behind it. Why was I doing this? Did that promotion actually fulfill me? Did the extra money make me feel fulfilled? Was it worth it?

## Being happy every day beats being happy "eventually"

You start to realize that being happy every day beats being happy, proverbially, in the future. Being happy doesn't always mean being comfortable or taking it easy. You could be working 18-hour days, or facing a massive rejection at work, but because you're chasing a goal you actually care about, you feel a *pump* -- and you're happy running that race. You let go of the delicious plate of pav bhaji in favor of working on your health. If you feel good about that decision, you're happy.

When I was preparing for JEE, I was in a zone where I let go of everything my friends were doing at the time for a single-minded pursuit. I wasn't miserable -- I was having the time of my life. There were days I felt sad, dejected, disappointed, or worried, but zooming out, that was a genuinely exciting part of my life.

**Caveat:** you can't blanket-apply this everywhere, all the time. Don't be stupid about it -- "my boss was a dick in a meeting, so I'm quitting my job" is not what I'm saying. If you see the same pattern holding over a long enough period (6-12 months), that's when you bring change. Everything goes through phases and cycles. It's okay to let something slip for a while, but keep an eye on it -- the longer it stays in that state, the more it hurts your long-term odds.

## Four pillars, from a documentary

After watching *Live to 100: Secrets of the Blue Zones* on Netflix, I felt like it distilled a long-term, compounding, happy life down to four things:

- Eat well
- Move a lot
- Have a purpose
- Have a community / friends

My translation of that:

- Take care of your health, don't feed it junk
- Exercise a lot
- Be pumped about something in your life
- Hang out with friends, laugh a lot, take care of your mental health

## Applying this now

Given where I am financially right now, and without a ton of responsibilities pulling at me, the way I want to live from here is simple: don't take any decision that would severely disrupt the four things above.

### Health

Create a lifestyle where you eat fresh, sleep enough, eat vegetables, eat good food. If your work or travel requires you to eat Maggi every night or eat out every day, it's not worth it -- you'll pay the cost when you're 60. Right now I'd call myself a 6/10 here. Push it to 8.

### Exercise

I've set a realistic goal of 100 workouts a year, plus a lot of day-to-day cycling and walking living in Amsterdam. Push it to 150 a year. Keep the active day-to-day lifestyle going -- don't get too sedentary.

### Be pumped

Don't stay in a job that makes you feel miserable. Do something with your life, day in and day out, where you feel *fuck yeah*. Chase something, go for a win -- it doesn't have to be work all the time. It can be a workout, a hobby, a trip you're preparing for. Just have something going on in your life that makes you want to get out of bed and say *fuck yeah*.

### Enjoy life

Don't work on weekends. Drink on Fridays, eat your favorite dessert, make fun of your friends, watch the movie you love. Sacrificing this isn't worth it -- what's the point of life otherwise? Go sit in the sun in a park. Telling yourself a story that all these sacrifices are for some greater good isn't worth it today -- enjoy your life. Positivity feeds back into everything else: it'll make you healthier, richer, and more productive. If your work already gives you enough energy that you don't feel sad, depressed, or like you're asking "why am I doing this," then you're already good.

## Counter: does this make you complacent?

There's a real counterargument here: to make an impact, you have to go all in. You have to sacrifice health, family, everything, to actually make a dent in the universe. Sacrificing one of these pillars in the short term can massively improve your baseline -- build a startup in your 20s, skip the friends, run yourself into the ground, and maybe you come out the other side a millionaire who can go hang out all they want.

## Counter to the counter

For most ambitious people, I think health, fitness, and relationships are the baseline you maintain -- and purpose is where you let yourself spike.

The way to treat career and life: get pumped about something, assuming your baseline is already sorted (thanks to privilege, luck, whatever got you there). Apply a basic greedy algorithm to life -- look for, or create, things you're pumped about. If your startup isn't going anywhere, pivot to a problem you're actually pumped about. You'll make it happen even if the odds are low. If you don't like your boss, change teams, change companies, change the problem you're working on. Being pumped and naturally giving 120% every day will compound more than any strategic decision made with your heart not in it.

If you maintain the four pillars, you'll last longer in the race. You won't burn out, you won't have a health scare, you'll have purpose, and you'll have people who care about you. Last man standing is a viable strategy. Do the right things for 40-50 years and it might not be the global optimum -- but it'll be a very high optimum, one you lived while having a great day, every day, with no regrets. You might start slower than the person who did the startup in their 20s, but you won't have picked up bad habits, you won't be overweight, you'll have a string of projects you were excited about and saw through, and you'll have experiences worth keeping. Life takes care of itself. You'll likely die more content -- and possibly more successful too, from compounding the right things day in and day out.
